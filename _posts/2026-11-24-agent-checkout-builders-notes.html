I listened to a Stripe discussion last week about AI agents that pay. I didn't want to just write about it, so this week I tried it on the sites I already run.

The rule I started with

The assistant never holds a card. Ever. When it wants to buy something, it creates a Stripe Checkout session on the server and hands the person the link. The person sees the price on Stripe's page and pays there. The price comes from the site's own settings, never from whatever the assistant typed. That one rule made everything else simpler, and it meant I could test with real people in the loop from day one.

What I built, and what's proven

Everything below ran in Stripe's test mode. No real money has moved yet.

  • Garage Door S'mores. It's my practice dessert site. I built a small checkout add-on I share across my sites, so any of them can offer an agent purchase. An assistant asked for a holiday pack, got a Stripe approval page, and I paid it with the 4242 test card. The receipt only came back after Stripe's signed webhook confirmed the payment, not when the assistant asked for it.
  • Garage Door Science. This one surprised me most. The site already diagnoses garage doors through its AI connection, so the assistant shows up holding the diagnosis. "A loud bang, one spring snapped" became a broken-spring call with a local pro and a $49 test deposit. Before I paid, the receipt check said unpaid. After, it said paid.
  • Super Intelligent Foo. Foo users can ask their assistant to upgrade to Pro. Foo also tells the assistant when someone hits a free limit, so the offer arrives at the moment it's useful.
  • Membership MCP. This is the MemberPress one, and it isn't built yet. I wrote about how we're thinking about it on the Membership MCP blog.

What surprised me

The hard part wasn't payments. Stripe's hosted checkout does the heavy lifting. The hard part was everything around it: making sure an assistant can't invent a price, that a receipt can't be faked by asking twice, and that a refund has a human path.

Some chat apps can't show a payment link at all yet, so the flow only works where the assistant can hand you a URL. That's a real limit, and I'm not going to paper over it.

And the sites that worked best were the ones that were already easy for an assistant to read. Clear names, prices in text, one obvious next step. That part you can do today without writing any code.

Where I think it goes

Next up are add-ons and domains on Foo, then a spending cap you set for an assistant that works while you sleep. Down the road, small payments for content and APIs might replace a lot of what ads do now. I'm not sure yet. I'll keep testing in the open and write up what breaks.

If you're building something similar, I'd like to compare notes.
