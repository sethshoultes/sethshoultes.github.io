My own website was the slow one. smartwebutah.com is where I have sent customers for years, and when I finally timed it, the page that explains what I do took 12 seconds to show its main picture on a phone. I keep telling other people to fix that. So I did it to mine, four ways, and wrote down every step.

The four versions

I left the real site alone and built each version next to it on its own address.

  1. WordPress as it was. 73 pages and posts, a shop, memberships, 12 online courses, a theme from 2023.
  2. A frozen copy. A tool visits every page like a visitor and saves it as a plain file. Nothing runs when someone visits.
  3. WordPress as the editor. I still write in WordPress. A small separate site reads my pages from it and shows them to visitors. People call this headless.
  4. A full rewrite. New design, new pictures, the old writing cut down to what was worth keeping.

What broke

A frozen page cannot remember anything, so anything that needs to remember has to go somewhere else.

  • The shop. 13 products and a cart. I dropped it. If your shop matters, keep WordPress or use a hosted cart.
  • Memberships. I looked at the real data before dropping them: five "active" subscriptions, all on the manual gateway, $1 paid in 90 days. Nothing was charging anyone.
  • The courses. 12 courses and 528 lessons. They moved to a free academy on this site, with every old address redirecting.
  • Comments, forms and search. There were 15 comments, no form and no search box, so there was little to lose. If you have them, hosted services do the job.

What the stopwatch said

I tested the same five pages on all four versions: the home page, the list of posts, a short post, the About page and the heaviest page. Each page got five runs on a phone and five on a computer, from the same Mac on the same network, and I show the middle result. The table is the phone test, as score out of 100, then seconds until the main picture shows.

On the heaviest page the main picture went from 12.2 seconds to 1.6. The page itself went from 2,117 kB to 94 kB.

  • The frozen copy was not the win I expected. It still carried the same big pictures, and on a phone its home page scored 63, below the original's 93. Freezing a heavy page gives you a heavy frozen page. It did answer in about 94 milliseconds against WordPress's 1,155 on a post.
  • The editor-stays version looks best, and I do not trust it. It scores 100 on four of five pages, but its pages are much plainer and several carry almost no pictures. It is not the same page, so the comparison is not fair.
  • The rewrite is the real result. It scored between 96 and 99 on every page, with the same words and a design I like better.

How I tested

Lighthouse 12.8.2, the engine behind PageSpeed, on its phone setting (simulated phone, slow connection) and its computer setting. Five runs per page per setting, back to back, the middle result shown. Run on 30 October 2026. The sites were measured one after another, not at once. This is lab data from one machine, not what real visitors saw, and WordPress is measured from its own server while the other three come from Cloudflare's network. A few points either way is noise. Every one of the runs, the script and the written method are in the repository.

Which one is right for you

Keep WordPress if you sell things, people log in or you change the site every day. Freeze it if the site is pages people read and it already looks right, but shrink the pictures first. Keep WordPress as the editor if you like writing in it and have someone to build the other half. Rewrite if the design is tired and much of the writing is padding. That last one took me a day, most of it deciding what to cut, and it was the one I would do again.
